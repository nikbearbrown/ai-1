# Chapter 8 — The Human Rewrite: The Seam

*The pipeline cannot enforce this gate. The author either does this work or the book is a Cowork dump with figures.*

**Capability — one line:** Students learn to read a Cowork draft as an author, identify what requires human judgment, and produce a revised draft that passes the Combined Test in their own voice.

**Learning objectives**

- (Evaluate) Score a chapter against all fourteen Combined Test items and identify the three lowest-scoring.
- (Apply) Rewrite a chapter's opening section to pass the four items the opening most depends on.
- (Create) Produce a complete revised chapter passing all fourteen Combined Test items in your voice.

## Opening — two paragraphs, no explanation

Read both. Then keep reading.

> **Version A.**
> Domain research is one of the most important steps in any
> textbook writing project. Without good research, your book
> cannot succeed. In today's rapidly evolving design landscape,
> understanding what AI is doing to your profession is essential.
> Studies have shown that 78% of design firms are integrating AI
> tools into their workflows. This means designers must adapt
> quickly. Many designers are finding that AI can help with
> brainstorming, mood-boarding, and initial concept development.

> **Version B.**
> Three LLMs answering the same question about your profession
> give you something no single LLM does: a map of agreement,
> divergence, and silence. Claude knows the published research.
> ChatGPT has read the trade press. Gemini has scraped the
> LinkedIn posts. Run the same prompt across all three on a
> Tuesday afternoon, and by Wednesday morning you have a domain
> research brief — the document Tic TOC will demand at /i1.

---

Both versions open the same chapter. Both are 75 words. One is the Cowork draft. The other is the author's rewrite. You knew which was which by the second sentence.

What you noticed, paragraph by paragraph:

- Version A opens in summary. Version B opens in a scene (Tuesday afternoon, Wednesday morning, three named tools).
- Version A's specificity is fake (78% with no source). Version B's specificity is operational (the three LLMs named, the timing real, the next chapter's command — `/i1` — set up structurally).
- Version A could be about marketing, education, small business. Version B could only be about a designer drafting their own AI+1 textbook.
- Version A ends gesturing. Version B ends pointing.

This is the chapter. Naming what you just noticed is the easy part. Producing Version B from Version A — for one chapter, then another, then twelve more — is the work this chapter teaches. It is also the work the model cannot do.

The reason the model cannot produce Version B is what Baldwin named in 1962: "The artist is distinguished from all other responsible actors in society... by the fact that he is his own test-tube, his own laboratory" [Baldwin, James, 1962, "The Creative Process," in *Creative America*; reprinted in *The Price of the Ticket*, 1985, St. Martin's]. The voice in Version B was paid for. Ten years of design practice, a thousand brand-pitch Tuesdays, the muscle memory of explaining a research process to a client over Wednesday-morning coffee — that is the laboratory. The model has not been to that laboratory. It can produce text *about* the laboratory; it cannot produce text *from* the laboratory.

This chapter is the seam where the laboratory enters the book.

## What the seam means — why this chapter is different

Every other chapter in this book describes a step in the pipeline. Run this command. Read this output. Evaluate against these criteria. The pipeline mostly enforces itself. Skip Chapter 5 and there is no scaffold. Skip Chapter 6 and the Chapter Writer refuses. Skip Chapter 7 and there is nothing to look at.

You can skip Chapter 8.

Nothing in the pipeline stops you. You can run the Chapter Writer in Chapter 7, accept whatever it produces, run the Finishing Pass in Chapter 9 over Cowork's prose, add the LLM enrichment in Chapter 10, and submit to KDP in Chapter 11. The build script will produce an EPUB. Kindle will accept it. You will have a book on sale within a week.

What you will not have is your book. You will have a Cowork dump with figures, indistinguishable in voice from any other Cowork dump produced anywhere else in your field this month. The fluency trap returns, this time at the level of the entire artifact. The book you produce by skipping this chapter is exactly the kind of generic output Chapter 1 taught you to catch in someone else's work.

This is the chapter the TIKTOC.md flagged as "must not read as a checklist chapter." It cannot, because if it does, it becomes the thing it warns against — a list of rules followed instead of a craft practiced. Lemov's framing in *Practice Perfect* is useful here [Lemov, Doug, Erica Woolway, Katie Yezzi, 2012, *Practice Perfect: 42 Rules for Getting Better at Getting Better*, Jossey-Bass]: the danger is "polishing what is easy to polish rather than what most needs polishing." A draft can pass a checklist and still be wrong. The chapter teaches the test as a scaffold the writer eventually outgrows, not as a scorecard the writer hands the model.

A note on what happens downstream if you skip. Chapter 9 adds figures to text. If the text is Cowork's, the figures decorate Cowork's voice. Chapter 10 adds LLM Exercises designed to be domain-specific — but the surrounding chapter is the calibration target. Generic chapter, generic exercises. Chapter 11 runs a fact-check and builds the EPUB. The EPUB validates. None of this catches the rewrite that did not happen. The pipeline is not designed to catch it. The author is.

## Five failure modes as rewrite targets

Chapter 7 named the five failure modes diagnostically — *here is what went wrong*. This chapter names them prescriptively — *here is what to do about it*. Same five modes. Different posture.

**Voice drift → specificity injection.**

What Cowork wrote: "In today's rapidly evolving design landscape, AI tools are reshaping how creative professionals approach their work."

What to do: cut the generality, replace with a specific moment. "It is 2:47 p.m. on a Wednesday. The client just shared a Midjourney image and asked if you can use it for the homepage." The specific defeats the generic. Joseph Williams's principle from *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* is the technical move [Williams, Joseph M. and Bizup, Joseph, 11th ed. 2014, *Style*]: lead with old information the reader can anchor on (the designer, the client, the Wednesday afternoon), then introduce new information (the Midjourney image, the question being asked).

**Fabricated specificity → cite, replace, or cut.**

What Cowork wrote: "Studies show 78% of design firms are integrating AI tools into their workflows."

What to do: one of three things, in order of preference. (1) Find the number in your pantry; if it is there and real, cite the source by title and date. (2) Replace with a number you can defend — "I count five major design tools that shipped generative features in 2024: Adobe Firefly, Figma AI, Canva Magic Studio, Photoshop's Generative Fill, and Midjourney's Style Reference." (3) Cut the sentence. Fabricated specificity that cannot be cited or replaced is decoration; the chapter is better without it.

**Missing domain judgment → embed one judgment per page.**

What Cowork wrote: "Designers must consider the implications of using AI tools in their workflows."

What to do: replace the sentence with a judgment only you can make. "I do not let Midjourney near a brand-identity exploration before the strategy phase is signed off. Generative tools widen the visual field too early and confuse stakeholders into thinking we are designing when we are still listening." One judgment per page, minimum. Not opinions about AI in general — judgments about your practice in particular. Baldwin's test-tube made operational.

**Padded middle → cut 25%, restructure remainder.**

What Cowork wrote: a 5,000-word chapter where the argument fits in 3,500 words.

What to do: read pages two through four with a pencil. Cross out any sentence that does not advance a claim, deliver an example, or name a trade-off. Now read what remains. Does it still hold together? If yes, the cuts stay. If no, the cuts revealed a structural problem the padding was hiding — go fix the structure. Sommers's 1980 finding is what makes this move efficient [Sommers, Nancy, 1980, "Revision Strategies of Student Writers and Experienced Adult Writers," *College Composition and Communication* 31(4)]: experienced writers revise at the level of argument and paragraph, not at the level of words. Cut 25% of words and the argument's actual shape shows up.

**Bridge questions that don't bridge → name the next chapter's deliverable.**

What Cowork wrote: "What does this mean for the future of design?"

What to do: open the next chapter's spec. Find what it actually delivers. Rewrite the bridge to name that thing. "Three LLMs is a research move. Tic TOC is the instructional architecture that turns the research into a book spec. Chapter 4 walks you through the session that produces your TIKTOC.md — the two hours that make the next four weeks possible." The bridge is a *commitment*, not a gesture. If the next chapter's spec is too vague to commit to, the problem is in TIKTOC.md, not in the bridge.

A sixth rewrite target, off the official list but worth naming: *sycophancy reversal*. Cowork drafts agree too easily with whatever the TIKTOC.md framed [Sharma, Mrinank et al., 2023, Anthropic, "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models"]. The "What would change my mind" section is partly designed to externalize the disagreement. When you rewrite, look for places the draft accepts a claim without testing it. Pushing back at one point per chapter, in your own voice, breaks the sycophancy spell.

## The Combined Test — fourteen items

`[verify — author to confirm canonical Combined Test items; the fourteen below are constructed from the five Cowork failure modes (Ch 7), Sommers's 1980 distinction between novice and experienced revision, Wiggins & McTighe's Stage 3 assessment principles, and standard pedagogical quality criteria. They are the working list this chapter draft uses. The canonical list goes in `_lib_combined-test.md`.]`

Fourteen items, in four groups: voice and specificity (1–4), structure (5–8), pedagogy (9–11), and authorial accountability (12–14). Each item is a scoreable question with a clear pass condition. The test is meant to be applied in twenty-minute sittings, not three-hour ones — the author at this point in the project is fatigued, and a checklist that takes a full afternoon does not get used.

**Group A — Voice and specificity (the four items the opening most depends on)**

1. **Scene-first opening.** Does the chapter open inside a specific moment — a named person doing a named thing in a specified time and place — rather than with a topic statement? *Pass:* a reader can answer "where am I, who is here, what is happening" by the end of the first paragraph. *Fail:* the first paragraph could be the abstract of an article on the topic.

2. **Domain-specific examples throughout.** Are at least 80% of examples from the book's actual target domain (not adjacent fields)? *Pass:* a peer in the field would recognize the examples as theirs. *Fail:* examples drift into "marketing," "small business," "education," or other domains the book is not about.

3. **Author voice consistent across the chapter.** Read the opening paragraph and the closing paragraph aloud. Do they sound like the same person? *Pass:* yes, both sound like the author. *Fail:* the middle or the end flattens toward magazine-article prose.

4. **No fabricated specificity.** Every percentage, every quoted study, every named report can be traced to a primary source in the pantry or by quick verification. *Pass:* every number defensible. *Fail:* at least one "studies show" or "78% of firms" with no traceable source.

**Group B — Structure**

5. **Mechanism before terminology.** Concepts are explained from first principles before their disciplinary name is introduced. *Pass:* a non-expert can follow the explanation; the term arrives as a label for something already understood. *Fail:* the chapter introduces the term first and then explains it, requiring the reader to hold an unfilled box.

6. **Named trade-offs.** At least one place per chapter where a choice's cost is acknowledged — what the choice rules out, what the alternative would have delivered. *Pass:* the reader sees the author thinking, not the author concluding. *Fail:* the chapter recommends without admitting cost.

7. **No padded middle.** Pages two through four advance argument, deliver examples, or name trade-offs in every paragraph. *Pass:* reading the middle aloud reveals no sentence whose absence would weaken the chapter. *Fail:* the middle contains restatement, summary, or connective tissue without load.

8. **Bridge question that bridges.** The chapter's closing question is answered (or directly set up) by the next chapter's spec. *Pass:* you can name where in the next chapter the answer arrives. *Fail:* the question is a topic heading shaped like a question.

**Group C — Pedagogy**

9. **Capability statement honored.** The chapter delivers what the TIKTOC.md capability statement promised it would. *Pass:* a reader who finishes the chapter can do (or evaluate, or analyze) the named thing. *Fail:* the chapter describes the topic but does not enable the action.

10. **Bloom's level achieved.** The chapter operates at the cognitive level its learning objectives claim. *Pass:* exercises at Apply level genuinely require application, not recall. *Fail:* the chapter says Evaluate and asks the reader to summarize.

11. **Exercises require the action they name.** Every exercise asks the reader to actually do something — not to "think about" or "consider," but to produce, identify, or evaluate. *Pass:* each exercise produces an artifact or a documented decision. *Fail:* exercises are reflection prompts dressed as work.

**Group D — Authorial accountability**

12. **Author judgment present.** At least one paragraph per chapter contains a claim only this author could have made — a judgment from practice, a counterintuitive position, a place where the author disagrees with received wisdom. *Pass:* a peer can point to the sentence and say "yes, that's [author]." *Fail:* every sentence could have been written by any competent commentator on the topic.

13. **Contested claims flagged or argued.** Where the field is split, the chapter either takes a position with reasoning or flags the dispute. It does not paper over. *Pass:* the reader can tell where settled science ends and contested territory begins. *Fail:* the chapter assumes consensus where there is none.

14. **What would change my mind.** The chapter contains, somewhere, a paragraph acknowledging the kind of evidence that would falsify its main claim. *Pass:* the author has thought about how the chapter could be wrong, and said so. *Fail:* the chapter reads as if the author cannot imagine being wrong.

**The two hardest to self-assess: items 3 and 8.**

Item 3 (author voice consistent) is hard because voice drift is gradual; you wrote the chapter, so you read it in your own voice even where the words are not yours. Three counter-moves: read aloud (voice drift is audible); put the chapter down for a week and reread (distance restores the ear); have a peer read just the first and last paragraphs and tell you if they sound like the same person.

Item 8 (bridge bridges) is hard because you are the author of both chapters and you know what is coming; the reader does not. Counter-move: read only the closing paragraph, then read only the next chapter's first page, with no memory of what's between. Does the bridge land? If you can't tell, give the two paragraphs to a peer.

Items 3 and 8 are where the chapter most argues against using the test as a checklist. Both require an outside ear. Designers have peers; this is what peers are for.

The Combined Test is a scaffold. Sommers and Saltz's longitudinal study of Harvard undergraduates [Sommers, Nancy and Saltz, Laura, 2004, "The Novice as Expert: Writing the Freshman Year," CCC 56(1)] found that the move from novice to expert was the move from rule-following to judgment. The test catches the failures the author can name. Voice, the irreducible thing, is not on the test. The author either supplies it or the chapter does not pass. Wiggins and McTighe's backward design [Wiggins, Grant and McTighe, Jay, 2005, *Understanding by Design*, 2nd ed., ASCD, Stage 3 on assessment evidence] gives the underlying principle: the test should produce the evidence the author would need to claim the outcome. Not a number; an artifact the artifact can be evaluated against.

## Worked example — one chapter rewrite, three passes

The chapter being rewritten is `ai-for-designers/chapters/03-domain-research.md`. The Cowork draft was annotated in Chapter 7. Here are the three passes.

**Cowork draft, opening paragraph:**

> Domain research is one of the most important steps in any textbook writing project. Without good research, your book cannot succeed. In today's rapidly evolving design landscape, understanding what AI is doing to your profession is essential. Studies have shown that 78% of design firms are integrating AI tools into their workflows. This means designers must adapt quickly.

**First-pass rewrite (structure fixed, voice generic):**

> Before you can write a chapter, you have to know what is true in your field. Domain research is the step that produces this knowledge. The three-LLM prompt — run the same question through Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini — is the method this book uses. Comparing three answers reveals where the field agrees, where it diverges, and where the model is making things up. The output is a research brief Tic TOC will read at /i1.

What changed from Cowork to first pass: the topic statement is gone; the fake number is gone; the structure of the method is named. What did not change: the voice is still generic. The paragraph could have been written by any competent technical author. Items 1, 2, 3, 4, 12 still failing.

This is the version most authors stop at. It feels finished because the worst failures are fixed. It is not finished. It is the *polish trap* — the productive feeling without productive output that Csikszentmihalyi might recognize as false flow [Csikszentmihalyi, Mihaly, 1990, *Flow*, Harper & Row]. The first-pass rewrite is the trap the chapter most warns against: improved enough to feel good, not improved enough to be yours.

**Second-pass rewrite (voice present, Combined Test passed):**

> Three LLMs answering the same question about your profession give you something no single LLM does: a map of agreement, divergence, and silence. Claude knows the published research. ChatGPT has read the trade press. Gemini has scraped the LinkedIn posts. Run the same prompt across all three on a Tuesday afternoon, and by Wednesday morning you have a domain research brief — the document Tic TOC will demand at /i1. The three answers will not match. That is the point. Where they agree, the field has settled. Where they diverge, you have something to ask a colleague about. Where they all hedge, you have probably hit a contested claim worth flagging.

What changed from first to second pass: the opening is now a scene (Tuesday, Wednesday, three named LLMs); the mechanism is explained at first principles (each LLM trained differently); a trade-off is named (three answers won't match — that is the point); one judgment is embedded ("worth flagging" is the author's call). Items 1, 3, 5, 6, 12 now pass. The paragraph is forty words longer than the first pass, but every word is doing work.

The delta between Cowork and first-pass is fixing failure. The delta between first-pass and second-pass is the chapter the book is actually about. Sommers's 1980 distinction maps directly: the first pass is novice revision (word-level, sentence-level); the second pass is experienced revision (argument-level, voice-level). The book this chapter teaches you to write is the second-pass kind.

Read Lamott on this if you have not [Lamott, Anne, 1994, *Bird by Bird*, Pantheon]. "Shitty first drafts" is the cultural permission slip that lets the author accept the Cowork draft for what it is — raw material for the work, not the work itself. The Cowork draft is the shitty first draft. The first-pass rewrite is the shitty second draft. The second-pass rewrite is the chapter.

## The rewrite loop — how many passes, when to stop

The worked example showed three passes for one chapter: Cowork, first-pass, second-pass. Three is descriptive, not prescriptive. Some chapters need two; some need five. The right number is "until the Combined Test passes and the chapter sounds like you when read aloud."

Three signals tell you to stop, and they have to all be present:

1. **The Combined Test passes** — all fourteen items.
2. **You can read the chapter aloud without flinching.** Voice drift is audible. If you flinch at a sentence, that sentence is not yet yours.
3. **A peer in your domain has read at least the opening and the closing.** This is the lightest possible peer review and the single most effective use of someone else's twenty minutes. Items 3 (voice consistent) and 8 (bridge bridges) need an outside ear.

There is a distinction the chapter has to be honest about: *finished* versus *avoided*. A chapter is finished when it passes the test and reads as yours. A chapter is avoided when you have stopped rewriting because you are tired and the chapter "is fine." The polish trap and the avoidance trap are different failure modes. The polish trap is over-revision of easy targets; the avoidance trap is under-revision because you cannot bring yourself to face item 12 (author judgment present) one more time.

The difference is not internal — it is external. A peer reader, even one, breaks the trap. So does the put-it-down-for-a-week test. So does reading aloud to anyone — partner, dog, voice memo for later playback. Bhat and colleagues at IUI 2023 documented that writers using LLM suggestions write *faster* but rate the output as less their own [Bhat, Advait et al., 2023, IUI 2023]. The same pattern operates in revision: the chapter feels faster to leave alone than to rewrite. The chapter is finished when you have done the work that feels slow.

A note on time. The author at this point in the project has been working for three or four weeks. Fatigue is real. Murray's process pedagogy is the philosophical license to be patient [Murray, Donald M., 1972, "Teach Writing as a Process Not Product," reprinted in *Cross-Talk in Comp Theory*, NCTE 2011]: drafts are not failures, they are stages. Rewrite in twenty-minute sittings, two chapters a day, over a week. The Combined Test is deployable in that rhythm. A three-hour rewrite session at the end of a long workday is exactly when missing-domain-judgment slips through.

## AI Wayback Machine — Toni Morrison

Wikipedia: "Toni Morrison."

Morrison (1931–2019) was the first Black woman to win the Nobel Prize in Literature. The novels — *Beloved*, *Song of Solomon*, *Sula* — are widely known. What is less widely known is that Morrison spent eighteen years (1965–1983) as a senior editor at Random House, where she shepherded books by Toni Cade Bambara, Angela Davis, Gayl Jones, and Henry Dumas into print. The work was largely invisible behind her novels.

Morrison's editorial career is the model for what the human author becomes in Chapter 8. Not a writer producing new text from a blank page — that was Chapter 7, sort of, with Cowork doing the producing — but an editor who is also the author, taking responsibility for the voice of the book. Morrison once said that her editorial work was about making the book "as much itself as it could be." That is exactly the work the seam asks of you. The Cowork draft is the book before it is itself. Your rewrite is the editing that lets the book be its own thing.

Morrison's 1993 Nobel lecture is the chapter's deepest claim, though the chapter only points at it: "We die. That may be the meaning of life. But we do language. That may be the measure of our lives." The reason the human rewrite is the gate is that the rewriting is doing language. The model is doing text. The two are not the same act.

**Try it:** Ask Claude: "Tell me about Toni Morrison's editorial work at Random House. What did she do that an AI cannot do — and what does that tell you about your own role rewriting a Cowork draft?"

## Exercises

**Exercise 8.1 (Evaluate).** Apply the Combined Test to one chapter from your `chapters/` directory. Score all fourteen items. For each, write the score (PASS / FAIL) and one sentence of evidence (the sentence in the draft that earned the score, or the absence that earned the fail). Identify the three lowest-scoring items. These are the rewrite targets for Exercise 8.2 and 8.3.

**Exercise 8.2 (Apply).** Rewrite the opening section of the chapter you just evaluated. The opening section must pass Combined Test items 1 (scene-first opening), 2 (domain-specific examples), 3 (voice consistent), and 13 (contested claims flagged or argued). Three to five paragraphs. Compare the rewritten opening to the Cowork draft side by side. Note in writing what changed and why.

**Exercise 8.3 (Create).** Produce a complete revised chapter passing all fourteen Combined Test items in your voice. For each item that failed in Exercise 8.1, write one sentence noting how it was addressed in the revision (which paragraph, what changed). Read the chapter aloud once. Note any flinches. Address the flinches. Then put the chapter down for at least twenty-four hours and read it again. Revise any sentence that does not sound like you.

This is the chapter's load-bearing exercise. The artifact it produces is the first chapter of your book that is *yours*. The remaining chapters follow the same pattern. The first one is the hardest. By the third, the rhythm is established. By the eighth, the Combined Test feels obvious.

## Still puzzling

- **What if the author genuinely cannot hear voice drift?** Many first-time authors cannot, especially in their own draft. Three counter-moves: read aloud (drift is audible even when invisible), wait a week and reread (distance restores the ear), have a peer read just the first and last paragraphs and ask whether the same person wrote both. The skill is trainable; the first chapter is the hardest because you have not yet learned what to listen for.
- **Is three passes the right number?** Descriptive, not prescriptive. The right number is "until the test passes and the chapter sounds like you." For most chapters in most authors' hands, three is the realistic count.
- **What about voice drift in the rewrite itself?** Real. Lee and colleagues at CHI 2022 measured it — writers using LLM suggestions converge on shared phrasings even when "rewriting." [Lee, Mina et al., 2022, "CoAuthor," CHI 2022.] The counter-move: rewrite in your own document, not inline in the Cowork draft. Open a blank file. Type the rewritten paragraph from memory of the argument, not from copy-paste of the original. The blank-page move breaks the homogenization spell.
- **Does the Combined Test apply to the frontmatter and back-matter chapters?** Partially. Items 1, 2, 3, 12, 14 still apply. Items 5–11 (structure and pedagogy) apply differently — frontmatter does not need a worked example. Use judgment. The test is a scaffold.
- **What if a chapter passes the test and the author still hates it?** The test catches the failures it specifies. It does not catch all failures. The author's persistent dissatisfaction is information. Investigate. There is usually an item the test should have, that this draft has not yet articulated. Add it.

## What would change my mind

The chapter's claim that the human rewrite is the gate rests on two pieces of evidence: Padmakumar and He's measurement of 10–20% lexical-diversity reduction in LLM-assisted writing [Padmakumar, Vishakh and He He, 2023/2024, ICLR 2024], and the visceral reader detection of "this reads like AI" that has emerged in the past two years. If diversity reduction drops near zero — if models genuinely stop converging on shared phrasings — the empirical leg of the argument weakens. If reader detection becomes unreliable (which it may, briefly, as detector accuracy worsens), the cultural leg weakens.

What does not change is Baldwin's claim — that the artist is their own laboratory. The work the model has not done in its life is the work it cannot produce in its prose. This is principled, not empirical, and I do not expect it to change. The pipeline can become better; the seam cannot become unnecessary. If anything in the next five years convinces me the seam is unnecessary, I will rewrite this chapter to say so. I am not expecting that draft.

## Bridge to Chapter 9

The text is yours. The chapter has passed the Combined Test, sounds like you read aloud, and has survived a week sitting in the drawer. The book is now stable in a way it was not at the end of Chapter 7. Voice is fixed; argument is fixed; structure is fixed.

Figures can now be added to stable text. This is the order the pipeline insists on. Adding figures to a Cowork draft is waste — the figures decorate prose that is about to change. Adding figures to a rewritten chapter is investment — the figures land on text that will still be there in the EPUB.

Chapter 9 runs the finishing pipeline. CAJAL produces figure briefs. SVGs become PNGs. The visual layer of the book arrives, and arrives in the right order.
